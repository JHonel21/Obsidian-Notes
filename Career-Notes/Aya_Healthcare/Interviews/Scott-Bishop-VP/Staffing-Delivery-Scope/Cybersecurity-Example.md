Mock Scenario #2: Cybersecurity Program Buildout

You’re supporting a pursuit with a regional health system (~5 hospitals, mixed inpatient and ambulatory footprint).

They’ve had:

- A recent security incident (not catastrophic, but exposed gaps)
- Increasing pressure from leadership and board to improve posture
- Fragmented tooling and no centralized security program

They’ve asked Aya to propose a 120-day engagement to:

- Assess current cybersecurity posture
- Establish a foundational security program
- Improve monitoring and incident response capabilities

They are:

- Cost-sensitive
- Under-resourced internally
- Not highly mature from a security standpoint

Your Task (Same Structure, Higher Bar)

1. Discovery approach (what you need to know quickly)

2. Workstreams (how you break this down)

5. Assumptions and risks

How to Think About It (Before You Answer)

- NIST CSF (Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover)
- Or practical equivalents:

- Assessment
- Controls

Don’t try to be a security engineer.

Be a solutions architect who can structure the engagement.

1. Discovery Approach

I’d approach discovery with a focus on quickly understanding current maturity, key gaps, and immediate risk exposure.

In the first 1–2 days, I’d align on three areas.

First, current-state capabilities across core domains like access control, monitoring, incident response, and governance. I’d anchor that loosely to a framework like NIST to ensure coverage.

Second, I’d review any recent incidents to understand where breakdowns occurred, whether in detection, response, or process.

Third, I’d identify constraints such as existing tools, internal skillsets, and any regulatory or compliance pressures.

The goal is to establish a clear baseline and prioritize the highest-risk gaps quickly rather than attempting a full deep-dive assessment upfront.

2. Workstreams

I’d break this into three primary workstreams aligned to building a foundational program:

Security Posture Assessment

A structured evaluation of current capabilities, risks, and gaps across key domains.

Program & Controls Development

Defining baseline policies, access controls, and governance structures needed to establish consistency and reduce risk.

Monitoring & Incident Response

Improving visibility through better use of existing tools where possible, and establishing clear incident response processes and playbooks.

I’d also include program management to coordinate across these areas and maintain alignment with leadership.

3. Staffing Model

I’d keep the model lean but ensure the right expertise is present early.

At a high level:

- 1 Program Manager to coordinate the engagement and maintain executive visibility
- 1 Senior Security Architect to lead assessment and program design
- 1–2 Security Engineers to support controls implementation and monitoring improvements
- Optional advisory support if deeper specialization is needed in areas like identity or network security

From a phasing standpoint, I’d front-load the senior architect during assessment and design, then shift more effort toward engineering during implementation.

The goal is to balance credibility with cost efficiency.

4. Delivery Framework

I’d structure this as a 120-day phased engagement:

Phase 1: Assessment (Weeks 1–3)

Rapid maturity assessment, gap identification, and risk prioritization.

Phase 2: Program Design (Weeks 3–6)

Define the target security framework, governance model, and prioritized roadmap.

Phase 3: Implementation (Weeks 6–14)

Execute on high-priority controls, improve monitoring, and establish incident response processes.

Phase 4: Stabilization & Handoff (Weeks 14–16)

Validate processes, refine controls, and ensure the client can sustain the program.

Throughout, I’d include regular governance with leadership and clear progress tracking tied to risk reduction.

5. Assumptions and Risks

Key assumptions would include:

- Access to existing tools and documentation
- Availability of internal stakeholders for validation
- Willingness to prioritize and act on identified risks

Key risks would include:

- Tool fragmentation limiting visibility improvements
- Lack of internal ownership post-engagement
- Attempting to do too much within a 120-day window

I’d mitigate that by prioritizing foundational capabilities and clearly sequencing the work.

6. Close (Important)

Overall, I’d position this as establishing a strong foundation rather than attempting full maturity in one engagement.

The focus would be on reducing the most immediate risks, improving visibility, and putting the structure in place for the client to continue maturing over time.

Key Components and Functions of NIST CSF 2.0:

- Core Functions:

- Govern: Establishes the organization’s cybersecurity risk management strategy, expectations, and policies.
- Identify: Develops understanding of the assets, systems, and risks that need protection.
- Protect: Implements safeguards (e.g., access control, training) to prevent or limit a cybersecurity event.
- Detect: Enables timely discovery of cybersecurity incidents through monitoring and analysis.
- Respond: Outlines actions taken when a security incident is detected.
- Recover: Restores assets and operations affected by incidents.

- Framework Tiers (1-4): Describe the maturity of an organization's risk management practices, ranging from Partial (ad hoc) to Adaptive (proactive).
- Framework Profiles: Help align the core functions with business goals, risk appetite, and resources.

How to Implement the NIST Framework:

- Prioritize and Scope: Identify business objectives and risk tolerance.
- Orient: Identify systems, assets, and regulatory requirements.
- Create a Target Profile: Define the desired cybersecurity outcome.
- Conduct Assessment: Evaluate current cybersecurity posture.
- Gap Analysis: Determine gaps between current and target profiles.
- Implement Action Plan: Prioritize and act on identified gaps.
